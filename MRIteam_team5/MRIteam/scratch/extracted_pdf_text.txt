Abstract. Accurate brain tumor classification via MRI is critical in
clinical oncology, yet conventional deep learning models suffer from severe
domain shifts, data leakage under slice-level evaluation, and high false-
negative rates. We present theMultimodal AI Consensus System
(MAICS), a three-tier arbitration architecture designed for privacy-
preserving, risk-mitigated diagnosis support. Tier 1 establishes a soft-
voting ensemble of ResNet50, EfficientNetV2, and DenseNet121 stabilized
by Bayesian Test-Time Augmentation. Tier 2 deploys YOLOv8 for spatial
region-of-interest localization. When these tiers disagree, Tier 3 condi-
tionally invokes a Vision-Language Model (Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite) as an
anatomically-constrained VLM arbitrator guided by spatial priors. We
further introduce anAnatomical Privacy Guardthat pre-filters non-
axial acquisition planes, acting as both a facial-privacy shield and a VLM
diagnostic-noise filter, reducing VLM API calls by 79.5% (12.14%→2.48%
of scans) while suppressing hallucination-induced errors. Evaluated on
3,461 test images (including 1,861 cases from Tam Tri Danang Hos-
pital, all tumour-positive; no-tumor controls are Kaggle-sourced—see
Limitations), MAICS achieved91.01% accuracyand92.04% Macro
F1, significantly outperforming the baseline CNN ensemble (McNemar’s
p = 0.000009). The system reduced the critical Glioma false-negative rate
from 0.85% (Tier-1 only) to0.43%(90.31% Glioma recall), suggesting
that multimodal consensus and risk-mitigation guardrails have potential
to reduce critical diagnostic errors in a controlled research setting.

1 Introduction
Glioma, Meningioma, and Pituitary tumors are primary brain neoplasms requiring
rapid, accurate MRI classification. Although Convolutional Neural Networks
(CNNs) show strong computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) performance, clinical
deployment faces three bottlenecks: (1) severe overfitting under scanner and
domain shifts [1,2]; (2) high risk of fatal false negatives in malignant lesions [4,5];
and (3) opaque “black-box” predictions lacking spatial interpretability [6,7,8].
We propose theMultimodal AI Consensus System (MAICS), a three-
tier diagnostic pipeline with risk-mitigation guardrails. Tier 1 (Bayesian Risk-
Calibrated Ensemble) combines ResNet50 [9] (trained with a cost-sensitive risk

2 Related Work
Computer-aided diagnosis for brain MRI has historically relied on CNNs and
ViTs, which overfit under domain shifts [1,2]. Existing CAD literature often uses
randomized slice-level splitting, causing data leakage that yields over-optimistic
performance (>99%) incapable of clinical generalization [3]. Spatial detectors like
YOLOv8 provide localization [7] but lack medical reasoning. Multimodal VLMs
like Gemini 3.1 [10] enable zero-shot reasoning [8], yet cloud APIs risk visual
hallucination and patient privacy leakage on non-axial scans. MAICS addresses
both gaps via a risk-calibrated consensus architecture with spatial detection and
privacy-guarded VLM arbitration under patient-level evaluation.
3 Proposed Method
MAICS operates as a three-tier diagnostic pipeline integrating CNN classification
ensembles, YOLOv8 spatial localization, and VLM (Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite) neu-
roanatomical reasoning. Tier 1 and Tier 2 run locally in parallel; upon diagnostic
conflict, an Anatomical Privacy Guard pre-filters non-axial planes (reducing cloud
VLM calls by 79.5%) before Tier 3 VLM arbitration is conditionally invoked
under strict risk-mitigation guardrails.

To validate generalization under domain shift, we compiled 7,200 Kaggle slices [15]

derive exclusively from Kaggle (400 test images), as Tam Tri contained no negative

3.4 Tier 2: YOLOv8 Spatial Localization
Tier 2 deploys a custom-trained YOLOv8m detector for independent spatial
localization, optimized with the standard YOLO multi-task loss (CIoU box
regression+BCE classification+Distribution Focal Loss) [ 7]. Beyond providing
an independent spatial class predictioncYOLO, the detector’s cropped bounding-
boxROIisforwardedtoTier3torestrictVLMattentionandpreventhallucination
on background noise.
3.5 Tier 3: VLM-Based Conditional Arbitration and Risk-Mitigation
Guardrails
To minimize latency (∼75ms fast path) and API cost, Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite is
invoked only upon diagnostic conflict:cBayTTA ̸= cYOLO, or Tier-1 confidence<
0.80(entropy>0.15).
Privacy-Filtered Arbitration.Upon conflict, the Anatomical Privacy Guard
first evaluates the acquisition plane. Non-axial scans (Sagittal/Coronal/Localizer)
block Tier-3 invocation, defaulting tocBayTTA. Confirmed axial slices forward
the Tier-2 ROI crop with anatomical prompt constraints to the VLM, yielding
verdict(c VLM, pVLM).
Risk-Mitigation Guardrails:
– Guard-1 (Dual-Tumor Agreement):If both Tier 1 and Tier 2 independently
detect a tumor, the VLM cannot override to “no tumor.”
– Guard-2 (Malignant Override Gating):If Tier 2 fails to localize a box, overriding
to “no tumor” requirespVLM ≥0.97.
– General Fallback:Otherwise, pVLM ≥ 0.85is required to accept cVLM; below
this threshold the system defaults tocBayTTA.
VLM Input and Data Governance.Tier 3 transmits three image views to the
Gemini API: (i) the full axial slice with the YOLOv8 bounding-box overlay (red
rectangle), (ii) an adaptive-margin ROI crop, and (iii) a2× zoomed view of that
crop. No DICOM metadata, patient identifiers, or demographic information is
included in the transmission. The Anatomical Privacy Guard blocks all non-axial
slices before transmission; only confirmed axial views are forwarded. Hospital
DICOM identifiers were replaced with anonymised numeric codes prior to any
research use, with the original mapping retained solely by the Tam Tri Danang
Hospital data custodian (retrospective study of anonymised diagnostic images;
IRB waiver confirmed by Tam Tri Danang Hospital Ethics Committee). The
Gemini Developer API (paid tier) was used under Google’s enterprise data-
handling terms, under which submitted prompts and images are not used to train
Google models.

3.6 Implementation Environment
MAICS was implemented in Python 3.10 (TensorFlow 2.15 for CNN backbones;
PyTorch/Ultralytics for YOLOv8m; OpenCV 4.8 for preprocessing) on an Intel
i5-12500H/16GB RAM/RTX 3050 (4GB VRAM) workstation—intentionally
edge-level hardware to demonstrate feasibility for low-resource clinical deployment.
Gemini API calls were routed through a multi-key proxy for rate-limit resilience.
End-to-end latency averaged 148.24ms/image (75ms fast-path for 97.52% of
scans;∼1.16s when Tier-3 is invoked).
4 Experiments
4.1 Hyperparameters and Training Settings
Data split: 80/10/10 train/val/test, enforced at the patient level. YOLOv8m:
AdamW, lr = 10−4, batch=4, 40 epochs. CNN backbones: Adam,lr = 10−4 (Co-
sine Annealing), batch=32, 25 epochs; ResNet50 trained with the risk-calibrated
loss (Sect. 3.3).
4.2 Benchmarking and Results Analysis
BayTTA integration yielded strong diagnostic stability: across the 3,461-image
test set, the Tier-1 ensemble alone achieved 89.92% accuracy/90.96% Macro F1.
The full three-tier system further reduced Fatal FNR (Glioma→No-Tumor mis-
classification) from 0.85% (Tier-1 only) to0.43%with active Risk-Mitigation
Guardrails (Guard-ON)—only 5 missed cases of 1,176 true Glioma scans, 90.31%
recall, a61.6% relative reductionversus the ResNet50 baseline (1.12% Fa-
tal FNR). This safety gain is directly attributable to Guard-1 and Guard-2
(Sect. 3.5); disabling both (Guard-OFF) raised Fatal FNR to 0.68% (8 cases),
confirming that consensus plus guardrails jointly suppress VLM hallucination-
driven errors.
Statistical Significance and Case-Level Analysis.At the image level, the
improvement from Guard-OFF to Guard-ON is statistically significant (McNe-
mar’s test: p = 0.000009, n01 = 14,n10 = 8,N = 3,461images). Since Kaggle
data lacks patient identifiers, a full case-level McNemar analysis is limited to
Tam Tri hospital cases (N=1,861 cases, one representative slice per case). At
this case level, the Guard-ON advantage does not reach statistical significance
(p = 0.091, n01 = 6, n10 = 2), consistent with the substantially reduced sta-
tistical power when moving fromN = 3,461images to N = 1,861cases. All
performance estimates with 95% Wilson confidence intervals are reported in
Table 2; source-stratified results are in Table 2. On the Tam Tri hospital subset
alone, MAICS achieved 86.57% case-level accuracy [95% CI: 84.94–88.04%] and
a hospital-validated Fatal FNR of 0.13% [95% CI: 0.02–0.73%] (1 missed Glioma
of 776 hospital Glioma cases).

4.3 Comparison with State-of-the-Art Studies
Table 3 compares MAICS with recent state-of-the-art methods. Although some
report near-perfect accuracy (>99%), they rely on randomized slice-level split-
ting, which leaks adjacent same-patient slices across train/test partitions and
inflates performance. MAICS strictly enforces patient-level splitting on real-
world hospital data. The system achieved 90.31% Glioma Recall; activating
Risk-Mitigation Guardrails reduced the critical Glioma false-negative rate from
0.68% to 0.43%. The no-tumor class achieved 99.25% Recall, but a domain
discrimination probe (AUC=0.991; Table 2) confirms that this metric is sub-
stantially source-confounded—see Limitations (Sect. 5) for full disclosure. The
most reliable hospital-scenario estimates derive from the Tam Tri-only subset
(86.57% accuracy, 89.69% Glioma Recall, 0.13% hospital-validated FNR on 1,861
tumour-positive cases; Table 2).
4.4 VLM Prompt Engineering Ablation
Prompt design was critical to arbitration reliability: free-form queries yielded
low, unreliable arbitration accuracy; adding explicit neuroanatomical grounding
rules improved reasoning validity; enforcing JSON-schema output eliminated all
parsing errors (100% success); and feeding quantitative uncertainty (Shannon
entropy) and YOLOv8 coordinates into the prompt raised conflict-resolution
accuracy to 76.81%. A calibration pitfall was also identified: an unconstrained
float confidence field caused artificially low VLM confidence (0.05–0.15) even on
correct predictions, triggering unnecessary Tier-1 fallbacks; scoping the schema
to realistic ranges (0.85–0.99 for confident cases) restored well-calibrated outputs,
allowing Guard-1/Guard-2 to function correctly and Fatal FNR to reach 0.43%.
4.5 Multimodal Interpretability Case Study
Fig. 2 illustrates MAICS’s interpretability for a challenging intra-axial lesion:
alongside the original slice, the Grad-CAM heatmap [6], and the YOLOv8 ROI,
the VLM outputs a structured Chain-of-Thought JSON citing specific anatomical
features (irregular borders, vasogenic edema, absent dural-tail sign) supporting
its Glioma verdict—replacing an opaque classification with a clinician-auditable
rationale.

a pre-screening triage layer that escalates uncertain cases to human review. The

three-tier architecture provides natural trigger points for Human-in-the-Loop
(HITL) intervention:
1. Tier-1 Uncertainty Flag.When BayTTA posterior entropy exceeds the
conflict threshold (H >0.15, corresponding to Tier-1 confidence< 0.80), the
case is flagged asuncertainand routed to Tier-3. In a clinical workflow, this
same flag can simultaneously route the case to a radiologist worklist, ensuring
that all low-confidence predictions receive mandatory human review before
any report is issued.
2. Guard Trigger.When either Guard-1 (dual-tumor agreement) or Guard-2
(low-box confidence override) is activated, the system has detected a potential
safety-critical scenario. These cases are prime candidates for immediate escala-
tion: the VLM’s Chain-of-Thought rationale (Fig. 2) is forwarded alongside the
Grad-CAM heatmap and YOLOv8 bounding box to the reviewing clinician,
providing full multimodal context rather than a bare classification label.
3. Radiologist Feedback Loop.Radiologist corrections on escalated cases can
be logged as structured feedback (class label + free-text note) to curate a
growing set of hard cases for active-learning fine-tuning of Tier-1 ensemble
weights—enabling continual improvement without requiring full retraining.
Escalation Rate Estimate.Tier-3 is invoked for2.48%of scans (post-Privacy
Guard); in a hypothetical HITL deployment, mandatory human review would
therefore cover approximately2.5–5%of cases—compatible with existing second-
read radiology protocols. The remaining≈ 95%of high-confidence cases would
proceed through automated pre-screening only. The HITL framework described
here has not been prospectively evaluated; clinical validation constitutes future
work.
5 Limitations and Threats to Validity
This study has four main limitations.First and most critical, the no-tumor
class is drawn exclusively from a public benchmark (Kaggle), while all tumour-
positive cases are hospital-sourced (Tam Tri). A domain discrimination probe
(logistic regression on ResNet50 penultimate-layer features, 5-fold CV) yielded
AUC=0.991 ±0.003, confirming that the representation nearly perfectly encodes
acquisition source. Accordingly, the overall no-tumor recall (99.25%) and Fatal
FNR (0.43%)cannot be interpreted as unbiased clinical estimates; the
hospital-only results (Table 2)—86.57% accuracy, 89.69% Glioma Recall, 0.13%
FNR [95% CI: 0.02–0.73%]—are the most reliable hospital-scenario estimates.

Prospective validation with hospital-derived negative controls is essential before

6 Conclusion
This paper presented theMultimodal AI Consensus System (MAICS),
a novel three-tier arbitration architecture engineered to address domain shift,
high false-negative rates, and the lack of interpretability in MRI-based brain
tumor classification. Evaluated on a unified 3,461-image test set under strict
patient-level splitting, MAICS achieved an overall diagnostic accuracy of91.01%
(3,150/3,461 correct) and a Macro F1-score of92.04%.
Active Risk-Mitigation Guardrails reduced the critical Glioma false-negative
rate from 0.85% to0.43%(5 missed tumors of 1,176 Glioma test cases)—a sta-
tistically significant49.4% relative risk reduction(McNemar’s p = 0.000009
at the test-image level). The VLM arbitrator resolved 76.81% of diagnostic con-
flicts (100.0% JSON parsing reliability), providing transparent Chain-of-Thought
reasoning. Source-stratified analysis (Table 2) and case-level confidence intervals
(Table 2) are provided to support appropriate interpretation under the dataset
constraints described in Section 5.
Scope statement.The system has been evaluated in a controlled research
setting using retrospective, anonymised data, and has not undergone prospec-
tive clinical validation or regulatory review; the reported results should not be
interpreted as evidence of clinical-grade diagnostic performance. Future work
includes: (1) prospective validation withhospital-derived negative controlsto
resolve source confounding; (2) locally-hosted open-source VLMs for full HIPAA
compliance; (3) multi-center validation; and (4) prospective HITL integration.
